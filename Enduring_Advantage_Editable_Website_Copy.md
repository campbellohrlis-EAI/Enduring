### How We Work

*   **Independent by structure:** No equipment sales. No referral fees. We do not profit from which solution wins, so the recommendation is honest by construction.

*   **The quarterback model:** Every account has one coach who quarterbacks it, drawing on the integrated platform and subject matter experts to deliver what the customer needs. *   **Thirty years of customer data:** Thirty years of independent energy and environmental management across North America, including operational data, measurement, and verification.

### The Process
**Tagline:** The process
**Heading:** The right things. In the right order. With the right partners. At speed.
**Intro:** Complexity is the enemy of adoption. Our process is designed to cut through the noise and deliver a clear, prioritised action plan, starting with The Diagnostic.
**Steps:**
*   **01 The Diagnostic:** A structured starting point. We start with whatever data you have available, build an initial baseline from it, identify the material opportunities, and hand you a ranked priority action before any ongoing engagement begins.
*   **02 Opportunity mapping:** Every lever is ranked by financial return and implementation effort. We identify the single highest-payback move for your specific circumstances, not a generic framework, but a costed, prioritised plan.
*   **03 Vendor-neutral selection:** We cover the entire sustainability field, energy, emissions, waste, water, supply chain, reporting. We do not install, sell equipment, or take referral fees. Our only income is the advisory fee you pay.
*   **04 Execution oversight:** Implementation is delivered by qualified vendors, generally sizeable firms built to carry that responsibility. Delivery responsibility and liability rest with them. We quarterback: one coach who owns the account, oversees the right order, the right places, and the right timing. You get specialist execution from parties built for it, plus an independent overseer on your side of the table.

### FAQ

**Q: Why is sustainability not a cost centre?**

#### The Four Data Problems CFOs Actually Face

EY's research identifies four specific challenges that make sustainability data indefensible.

**Meter-level data collection.** Not utility bill aggregates. Not engineering estimates. Actual consumption data from the point of use, collected at a frequency that allows anomaly detection.

**A quality-control layer.** A process that flags data that falls outside expected ranges, identifies missing records, and requires resolution before the data is included in the baseline.

**An audit trail.** Every number in the baseline traceable back to a primary source, a meter reading, a utility invoice, a waste manifest, with a documented chain of custody.

**Q: What is the difference between carbon risk and nature risk?**

Carbon risk is largely global and can be measured in a single unit (CO2e). #### The Three Types of Sustainability Consulting

To understand the value, you have to understand the market. There are three main types of sustainability consulting.

**Reporting and Disclosure:** These firms help you meet the requirements of frameworks like CSRD, SEC, or TCFD. The value is compliance and risk mitigation. It's a necessary cost, like a financial audit, but it rarely drives operational improvement.

**Strategy and Frameworks:** These firms help you set targets, define your materiality, and build a high-level roadmap. The value is alignment and communication. It's useful for the board and for investors, but it often lacks the granular detail required for execution.

**Material Results Advisory:** This is where the real ROI lives. These firms focus on identifying and delivering specific, measurable improvements in operational performance, energy efficiency, and supply chain resilience. The value is direct financial return.

An engagement that unlocks a 30-basis-point pricing advantage on a $200 million loan has a clear and compelling ROI. An engagement that secures a major customer contract by providing auditable Scope 3 data has a clear and compelling ROI.

#### The Cost of Doing Nothing

When evaluating the cost of consulting, it's essential to consider the cost of doing nothing. The cost of missed operational efficiencies, the cost of higher insurance premiums, the cost of restricted access to capital, and the cost of losing customers to more transparent competitors.

In many cases, the cost of doing nothing is far higher than the cost of a high-quality advisory engagement. The key is to ensure that the engagement is focused on the things that materially drive value for your specific business.

#### What Good Looks Like

A high-quality sustainability consulting engagement has a specific structure. It starts with a clear business problem, not a generic scope of work. It uses an evidence-led process to identify the highest-return opportunities. It provides a ranked action plan with financial estimates for each initiative. And it stays close to implementation to ensure the intended results are delivered.

If you are considering a sustainability engagement, ask the advisor to show you the ROI of their past work. Ask them to explain their methodology for ranking initiatives. And ask them to commit to a first phase that produces a recommended action, not just a framework.

Sustainability consulting is worth the investment when it is treated as a financial discipline, not an environmental exercise. The companies that understand this are the ones that are building an advantage that endures.

## What Questions Should I Ask a Sustainability Consultant?

**Category:** CFO Strategy

**Author:** Campbell Ohrlis (VP, Revenue, Data & Innovation)

**Author Bio:** Campbell Ohrlis is VP at Enduring Advantage, focused on commercial strategy and the growth of the firm. He works with CFOs and operational leaders to connect environmental decisions to commercial priorities. Enduring Advantage does not sell equipment or take referral fees. **Excerpt:** The first consultation should help you decide whether the advisor can identify the right first action, explain why, and stay close enough to delivery to make it real.

**Tags:** CFO Strategy, Advisory, Due Diligence, Financial Return, Materiality, Hiring & Advisory

### FAQ

**Q: What is the most important question to ask a sustainability consultant?**

Ask what your organisation should do first and how the advisor arrived at that view. A strong answer identifies one material action, explains the alternatives screened out, and makes the financial and environmental case visible together.

**Q: Should I expect a complete assessment on day one?**

No. Most organisations start without complete data. A credible advisor should be clear about what can be learned from available data now, what needs to be refined later, and what a practical first phase will produce.

**Q: How can I assess an advisor’s independence?**

Ask whether the firm sells equipment, earns referral fees, or has ownership or affiliate relationships that could shape its recommendation. These structures are not automatically disqualifying, but they should be disclosed clearly so the decision-maker can understand incentives.

**Q: What should happen after the strategy is delivered?**

Someone should own vendor selection, schedules, measurement, and the practical work required to turn a recommendation into an implemented result. If the advisor does not stay involved, leadership needs to know who will quarterback that responsibility internally.

### Content

Climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution are forcing action from mid-market companies at exactly the moment margins are thinning. That pressure is real. What surprises many executives is that the hard part is no longer finding solutions.

There are thousands of credible technologies, programmes, and services available. Each can work somewhere. The chance that any single vendor’s answer is the best one for your building portfolio, production line, and balance sheet is much smaller. Complexity is the barrier, not a lack of options.

The job of a first consultation is not to be sold. It is to find out whether the person across the table can help you do the right things in the right order.

#### What Should We Do First, and How Did You Arrive at That?

This is the most important question you will ask. A weak answer comes back as a list: many opportunities, a scoring matrix nobody can explain, and a recommendation to start a working group.

A strong answer names one thing. It explains what was screened out and why, and it shows financial return and environmental benefit side by side. If a consultant cannot tell you what is not worth your attention, the materiality screen is not doing its job.

#### What Can You Tell Us Before We Have Good Data?

Be cautious of anyone who promises a complete assessment of your operations on day one. Almost no company has complete data at the start. The honest answer sounds like sequencing: start where useful data or funding already exists, get a first result on the board, and let that result earn the right to collect and refine more data.

Ask what the first ninety days actually produce. You want a decision and a next step, not a long period spent building a data set before anything becomes actionable.

#### Do You Sell Equipment, or Do You Take Referral Fees?

Ask both parts. Plenty of firms describe themselves as independent while collecting a percentage from the vendors they recommend. Independence is not a promise. It is a structure. Either the firm has a financial interest in a particular product being purchased or it does not.

A yes is not automatically disqualifying. It does mean the recommendation list may have a shape you can predict, and a second opinion can be sensible before capital is committed.

#### How Are You Paid, and What Happens if the Result Does Not Appear?

Commercial structure tells you where a firm’s attention is likely to go. Ask which parts of the work are covered by the advisory agreement, what specialist costs are passed through, and what will be measured after a recommendation is implemented.

Then ask the follow-up: who verifies the result, against which starting point, and over what period? Specialist engineering and implementation support can be necessary. What matters is that the role, cost, and accountability are disclosed before the work begins.

#### Who Does the Work, and Who Checks It?

Technology can accelerate analysis and drafting, but an output has not stood in your mechanical room, looked at an equipment replacement cycle, or checked whether a regional contractor has capacity. Ask who reviews the work before it reaches you, what practical expertise they bring, and how the recommendation is tested against your operating reality.

Speed is useful only when it is paired with judgement. A fast document is not the same thing as a plan that works in the real world.

#### If the Incentive Disappeared, Would This Still Make Sense?

Grants and rebates are real money and they should be used. The risk is building a programme that only works because of them. Funding changes with governments and budget cycles, and a plan underwritten only by a subsidy carries that expiry date inside it.

Ask for the economics with the incentive removed. Some projects will still clear your hurdle rate. Those are the durable ones. An advantage built on a subsidy will expire. One built on economics will endure.

#### What Happens After the Report?

This is where much sustainability spending quietly dies. A firm delivers a good strategy, the deck goes into a folder, and months later someone asks what happened to it.

Find out whether the advisor stays close to implementation. Who runs selection, manages the schedule, and reports on whether the intended value actually appears? Somebody has to quarterback the work to done. If it is not the advisor, it is you.

#### What Have You Told a Client Not to Do?

This is one of the hardest questions to fake. An experienced advisor should be able to describe a project that did not make sense, a certification that was not material, or an initiative that should have waited for an equipment replacement cycle.

If the answer is vague, or every past engagement apparently went perfectly, you may be listening to a salesperson rather than an advisor.

#### Who Owns You, and What Else Do You Own?

Ask about ownership, affiliates, and any software the firm licenses from a related company. Related-party arrangements are common and are not a problem on their own. Undisclosed ones are. A firm that volunteers this without being pushed is telling you something useful about how the rest of the relationship may go.

#### What Good Looks Like

You should leave a first consultation knowing what your next best action probably is, what data would confirm it, roughly what it is worth, and what the first phase would require. If you leave with only a proposal for discovery and a promise of a framework, you have spent an hour learning very little.

The right advisor does not need to pretend certainty where data is incomplete. They need to show you how to move from the available evidence to a better decision, then stay human and accountable enough to make it real.

## How Long Does a Sustainability Project Take?

**Route:** /how-long-does-a-sustainability-project-take

**Subtitle:** From triage to verified result: a realistic timeline for corporate environmental performance.

**Category:** Advisory & Delivery

**Date:** August 24, 2026

**Author:** Rob Kirkby (Principal, Enduring Advantage)

**Excerpt:** The time it takes to see a result from a sustainability project depends on the complexity of the operation and the state of the data. Here is a realistic breakdown.

**Tags:** Timeline, Implementation, CFO Strategy, Operational Efficiency, Project Management, Hiring & Advisory

### Content

One of the most common questions we get from CFOs is: "How long is this going to take?" They want to know when they will see the first savings, when the first disclosure will be ready, and when the programme will become self-funding.

The answer depends on the complexity of the business and the current state of its data. But for a typical mid-market enterprise, there is a consistent timeline from initial triage to verified result.

#### Phase 1: Triage and Prioritisation (2-4 Weeks)

The first phase is about identifying the highest-return opportunities. It begins with a 30-minute triage to assess the operational footprint and identify the most significant environmental dependencies. This is followed by a more detailed assessment of the top 20 percent of locations by capital exposure.

The output of this phase is a ranked action plan, identifying the single next action worth examining and the financial case for doing so. This phase is fast because it uses available data and expert judgement to narrow the field.

#### Phase 2: Deep Dive and Baseline Verification (4-8 Weeks)

Once the next action is identified, the second phase is about building a defensible case for investment. This involves collecting meter-level data, verifying the operational baseline, and conducting a detailed technical and financial evaluation of the proposed initiative.

The output of this phase is a board-defensible investment proposal, including a clear payback period, a projected GHG reduction, and a plan for implementation. This phase takes longer because it requires high-quality, auditable data.

#### Phase 3: Implementation and Execution (3-12 Months)

The third phase is where the work gets done. The timeline for implementation depends on the nature of the initiative. A simple process change or procurement renegotiation can be completed in a few months. A major equipment retrofit or supply chain transition can take a year or more.

During this phase, the focus is on managing vendors, maintaining the schedule, and ensuring that the work is completed to the required standard. This is where the accountability gap is most likely to appear, and where a dedicated quarterback is most valuable.

| Phase | Timeline | Key Deliverable |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Triage & Prioritisation | 2-4 Weeks | Ranked Action Plan |
| Deep Dive & Baseline | 4-8 Weeks | Investment Proposal |
| Implementation | 3-12 Months | Completed Initiative |
| Measurement & Verification | Ongoing | Verified Result |

#### Phase 4: Measurement and Verification (Ongoing)

The final phase begins as soon as the initiative is implemented. It involves measuring the actual results against the verified baseline and reporting the findings to leadership. This is essential for confirming the ROI of the investment and for meeting disclosure requirements.

In a profit-first programme, the savings from the first initiative are used to fund the next one, creating a self-sustaining cycle of improvement. The programme doesn't end; it becomes a permanent part of the operational discipline of the business.

#### The Realistic Summary

You can have a ranked action plan in a month. You can have a defensible investment proposal in three months. And you can see the first verified results in six to eighteen months.

The companies that try to move faster often do so at the expense of data quality and defensibility. The ones that move slower often get bogged down in discovery and never reach the execution phase. The key is to maintain a steady, disciplined pace, focusing on one material action at a time.

In a profit-first programme, a sustainability consultant plays four distinct roles, each focused on a material driver of enterprise value.

The first role is identifying and quantifying the environmental risks that are currently invisible to the business. This includes physical risks like location-specific extreme weather and sea-level rise, as well as transition risks like changing regulations and shifting customer requirements.

The second role is finding the untapped operational efficiencies hidden in the company's environmental data. This is about identifying where energy, water, and raw materials are being wasted and how that waste can be reduced or eliminated.

An advisor uses intensity benchmarking and technical expertise to identify high-ROI operational improvements. They look past the aggregate utility bills to find the specific motors, boilers, and processes that are underperforming. The third role is helping the company access the growing market for sustainability-linked financing. This involves building the audit-ready data baseline that lenders and investors require and helping the company set and track material performance targets.

An advisor understands the requirements of the capital markets and can help the CFO navigate the complex landscape of green bonds, sustainability-linked loans, and transition finance. The goal is to lower the company's cost of capital and improve its access to liquidity.

#### The Execution Partner

The fourth, and often most important, role is staying close to the work until the results are delivered. This is about closing the accountability gap between strategy and execution.

An advisor acts as a dedicated quarterback, managing vendors, tracking schedules, and ensuring that the intended value of each initiative actually appears. The real power of a sustainability consultant comes when these four roles are integrated into a single, cohesive discipline. When risk management informs efficiency improvements, and efficiency improvements drive capital allocation, and capital allocation is supported by rigorous execution.

If you are looking for a sustainability consultant, don't just look for a reporting assistant. Look for a partner who can help you build an advantage that endures across every dimension of your business.

**Q: What should we prepare before hiring a sustainability consultant?**

Technical specialists, reporting and disclosure firms, broad professional-services teams, and orchestrators can all be useful. They are not interchangeable. The right choice depends on whether you already know the project or need help deciding what should happen first.

A defined first deliverable, a transparent starting-point method, clarity about specialist costs, data ownership and confidentiality, a clear exit point, and a practical test of whether the work changed the next decision.

### Content

Most companies run this process backwards. They write a broad scope of work, send it to several firms, compare the proposals, and select the one that looks most thorough. Months later they may have a report and still not know what to do first.

Before contacting anyone, write down the decision you actually need made. Not “develop a sustainability strategy.” Something closer to “we have capital available and do not know which projects deserve it,” “our customer requirements cannot be answered,” or “our energy costs are rising and nobody can explain where the exposure is.”

That sentence does more work than a lengthy request for proposal. It tells a serious firm what you need and lets it say quickly whether it can help.

#### Inventory What You Already Know

Spend a short period finding the information that exists: utility records, equipment details, metering, water and waste invoices, previous audits, fleet records, and production volumes. The data will almost certainly be incomplete. That is normal.

What matters is the distinction between what an advisor can tell you in the first phase and what needs to be refined later. A firm that promises a complete assessment of everything immediately is not being realistic about the work.

Technical specialists, reporting firms, broad integrated teams, and independent orchestrators all do different work. Hire a specialist when you already know the project. Use reporting support when a customer or regulator requires a particular document. Consider an orchestrator when the harder issue is deciding what goes first and coordinating the specialists after that decision is made.

The question is not which category has the best credentials. It is which form of help matches the decision your business needs to make.

#### Run the First Meeting as a Test

Do not let the first meeting become a capabilities presentation. Bring operations and finance, then ask what the firm would screen out, what it would do before the data is complete, what it has told a client not to do, and who will review the analytical output before it reaches you.

Ask directly whether the firm sells equipment, takes referral fees, or has affiliated software and ownership relationships. Related arrangements are not automatically a problem. Lack of disclosure is.

#### Structure the First Phase So It Can Prove Itself

Start with a defined first deliverable that includes a recommended action and its expected value, not just a framework. Agree the starting-point method before savings are claimed. Be clear about specialist and implementation costs, data ownership, confidentiality, and the decision point at which you can continue or stop.

A first phase should change what you do next. If it produces only a ranked list and a request for more discovery, you bought a report. If it produces one action, its rationale, the evidence behind it, and a plan someone is accountable for running, you bought the thing you came for.

#### Staff Your Side of the Table

The engagements that falter often do so internally. Name an executive sponsor with authority, a data owner who can provide records without a long queue, and a finance partner who agrees the starting-point method early.

The advisor can bring a process and an outside perspective. Your organisation still has to make the decision and create the conditions for the work to land.

## Article Body Content

### Section: Introduction
Everything that makes environmental decisions difficult inside your own four walls gets harder across a supply chain. You own less of the data, less of the equipment, and less of the decision. Costs, exposure, and emissions sit across suppliers, lanes, and facilities that belong to other businesses.

That is precisely why prioritisation matters. The job is not to send the same request to the whole supply base. It is to identify the suppliers, lanes, and contracts where a decision can change the outcome.

### Section: Why this is arriving on the leadership agenda now
Customer requirements are moving down supply chains. Large buyers in automotive, retail, food, and government increasingly ask suppliers to explain exposure, emissions, and improvement plans. A weak answer can become a revenue and relationship issue before it becomes a reporting issue.

Physical exposure is also material. CDP reported in May 2026 that 11,261 companies disclosing full environmental data through its 2025 questionnaire anticipated US$714 billion in future financial impacts from extreme weather. It attributed US$326 billion of that anticipated exposure to reduced production capacity, and reported that 48% of identified risks were expected within two years. These are system-level figures, not an individual-company forecast, but they make the business case for examining critical dependencies more concrete.

A 2024 Nature study led by researchers at King’s College London and Tsinghua University estimated that climate-disrupted global supply chains could add US$3.75 trillion to US$24.7 trillion in net economic losses in adjusted 2020 dollars by 2060, depending on the emissions pathway. The implication for an individual company is not to model the global number. It is to understand where its own dependencies are concentrated.

### Data Matrix: Extreme Weather & Supply Chain Impact

- **US$326B**: anticipated impacts linked to reduced production capacity in CDP’s analysis

### Section: The filter that makes the work tractable
Apply two questions to each supplier, category, and lane. First: is it material? Consider spend, volume, emissions intensity, production criticality, and exposure to disruption. Second: do you have leverage? Consider your share of the supplier’s revenue, the timing of the contract, viable alternatives, specifications, and what the supplier needs from the relationship.

Material exposure with leverage is where action belongs. Material exposure without leverage may call for alternatives, resilience planning, or a different sourcing strategy rather than another questionnaire. Exposure that is not material should not consume scarce operating attention. This filter gives procurement and operations a working set they can explain.

### Section: Where value often turns up
Freight mode, routing, consolidation, load factor, backhaul opportunities, and network design can affect cost and emissions together. Packaging can influence freight cost and material use, especially where specifications no longer match the product or route. Supplier energy intensity can be a sourcing, cost, and resilience signal when comparable production data is available.

Concentration and location risk can be equally important. A single-source component from a facility exposed to flooding or water stress is a resilience problem with a financial consequence. Contract terms and renewal calendars can be practical leverage points. Joint improvement projects can make sense where a long relationship and commercial leverage allow the parties to share a credible benefit.

### Section: Handle data honestly and in phases
Supplier-specific primary data is valuable, but many suppliers do not yet have it in a comparable form. Start with the procurement, freight, and operating records you already have. Use estimates for a broad first screen, label the confidence clearly, then seek better information from the suppliers and lanes that the materiality-and-leverage filter surfaced.

The first result should earn the access for the next round. A broad survey before the decision criteria are clear can create work without improving a decision. A phased approach directs deeper data collection to the places where it can change a sourcing, logistics, or contract choice.

### Section: Where AI helps, and where a human check still matters
Generative tools can accelerate supply-base mapping, lane screening, first-pass emissions estimation, and supplier-engagement drafts. That changes what is practical to analyse. It does not know whether an alternative supplier has a long qualification cycle, whether a mode shift breaks a service commitment, or whether a carrier’s regional capacity has changed.

Every draft needs a human sanity test before it reaches procurement. Speed matters. So does operating context.

### Section: What an early supply-chain decision phase should produce
A useful first phase produces a spend-and-exposure map that is good enough to rank rather than to report. It identifies a visible working set of material suppliers and lanes, names what was screened out, and recommends a next action with stated assumptions. It can also map contracts and renewals, and create a short list of concentrated dependencies that deserve attention.

The aim is not a complete inventory before action. It is a defensible first move that improves the next decision.

### Section: What to ask before you buy help
Ask which suppliers and lanes are material, what was screened out, and what can be learned before new supplier data arrives. Ask which changes may affect cost as well as emissions, where single-source exposure exists, who reviews the analysis before procurement sees it, and whether the advisor sells products, software, or referral arrangements.

The final question is what happens after the recommendation. Supply-chain change touches procurement, logistics, production, and suppliers at once. Someone has to hold the work together to done.

## FAQ: Supply Chain Sustainability
### Question: What should a company prioritise first in supply-chain sustainability?
**Answer:** Start with material exposure and leverage. Look for suppliers, lanes, categories, or single-source dependencies that matter to spend, volume, operational criticality, emissions intensity, or physical risk, then assess whether a commercial action is possible.

### Question: Can supply-chain action begin without complete supplier data?
**Answer:** Yes. Spend-based estimates and existing procurement, freight, and operational records can provide a useful first screen. Primary data is most valuable once the material working set is clear.

### Question: Which supply-chain changes can affect both cost and emissions?
**Answer:** Freight mode and routing, load factor, packaging design, supplier energy intensity, sourcing concentration, contract terms, and joint improvement projects can each affect cost, resilience, and emissions. The right choice depends on the operating context.

### Question: Why should contract and renewal dates be part of the analysis?
**Answer:** Commercial leverage changes over time. A renewal, sourcing decision, or specification change can create a practical point to set requirements or evaluate alternatives without treating supplier engagement as a separate compliance exercise.
